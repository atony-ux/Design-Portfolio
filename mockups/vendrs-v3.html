CONTEXT

Five designers, eight weeks, one prompt

Design Interactive asked how we might help small business owners operate, share resources and connect with their community. We narrowed "small business" to the people we could actually learn from: UC Davis student owners selling at pop-ups and night markets.

RESEARCH

They didn't lack ambition. They lacked infrastructure.

20+ survey responses showed how common each problem was; 10 interviews with art, food and service owners showed why it mattered. Discovery was accidental: owners heard about markets through a friend's story, usually after applications closed.

97%want a bigger audience for what they make
80%run the entire shop by themselves
70%want to scale, with no system to do it

Owners found markets through Instagram stories, too late to apply.

FEATURE 01 · ONBOARDING

Ask for less, and make every answer work twice

Owners abandon tools that demand setup before showing value, so I kept onboarding to four steps and gave each one a job beyond collecting data.

✕ Rejected

One long profile form

Complete, but it front-loads the work and reads as paperwork before any value arrives.

✓ Shipped

Four steps, one decision each

Social login opens it, so the highest-friction moment lands before we ask for trust.

FEATURE 02 · EVENTS MAP

Discovery you can see, sorted by what you can reach

Discovery was the loudest pain in research, so I made it spatial. Pins show what is around you, a side panel lists the same events for scanning, and the filters are the business types from onboarding, so a food vendor lands on food events with zero setup.

Selecting an event in the list highlights its pin, and the filters come straight from onboarding.

✕ Rejected

Another events feed

Familiar, but it buries the thing owners judge an event on: can I physically get there.

✓ Shipped

Map and list as one surface

Two views of one set, synced both ways, sorted by distance and filtered by business type.

WHAT TESTING CHANGED

Two of the three changes landed on my features

  • Events Map. Testers read the map and the list as separate tools, so I tightened the sync until selecting in one obviously drove the other.
  • Onboarding. I cut the step people called busywork and led with value instead, which lifted completion.
  • The customer flow (team). A second audience needed explaining, so we cut it and pointed the product at owners only.
TAKEAWAY

The boring early decision was the one that compounded

Business type looked like a form field. It became the backbone of discovery: onboarding did real work instead of collecting data, and the Events Map inherited personalisation for free. Next I would add messaging so owners can mentor each other, and build the customer-facing side to drive foot traffic back to the vendors.
